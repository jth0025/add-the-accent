Do you hear that??

A scene freezes, dims, goes translucent. Somewhere beneath it, a sound is rising — soft at first, then insistent, until it isn't background anymore. It's now the plot twist of an action thriller. Piercing. Intentional. The scene dissolves entirely and I'm awake. My phone is close to my head this time. Faithfully, its proximity has been relatively close for the past eight months now. Every morning arrives the same and yet distinct, folded into whatever version of the day is waiting outside my window. The sky is always rust-colored at this hour, and somewhere close, birds are already arguing about who owns the morning.

I live in the building I manage. Well — one of them. My office sits across the street, a separate address, a comfortable proximity; a separate ledger of hours. But here, in the building itself, there's a different kind of clearance. Fewer walls between the job and the life. It should feel like no boundary at all, and some mornings it doesn't. But most mornings it feels like the safest place I've ever lived — comfortable in the way someone is comfortable in a home they built the locks for themselves: at ease, but never fully off duty.

And now — a mask, of sorts.

I don't wear one actually. That's the twist. My power is that I look like nothing at all. No cape. No symbol stitched to my chest. Just a man walking a property at six in the morning before anyone else is up, checking stairwells, testing locks, listening for the small sounds that mean the building is breathing wrong or not.

This isn't a horror story. I want to be clear about that, because it could read like one if you squint. The horror-movie landlord is the absent one — Doctor Unreachable, a name on a check nobody's cashed, a voicemail box that's always full. The Landman is his inversion. He's always there; mostly there. Which means he's always accountable. Always. There's no version of the story where he doesn't pick up; mostly.

He is, in his own quiet way, a kind of super; always.

Not strength. Presence. That's the power, if it has to be named — showing up, reliably, in a genre built almost entirely on absentee villains and faceless management companies. My superpower is being reachable: answering the phone at eleven at night on the company line, remembering every tenant's name, remembering that the kid in 505 has asthma, so the mold complaint moves to the top of the list without needing to be asked twice.

Then there's 817. Behind on rent again. I listen to the story — I always listen to the story. I relate. That's easy. I was always them. — but listening doesn't erase what's due. Eventually they have to receive it: I dropped my head to ensure I had all 4 of them in my folder. The Notice. The three days, and after that, the ground can open. But the countdown isn't the whole truth of it. Communicated with married excellence, honestly enough, it can cast a heavenly essence. A kind of spell, if you want to call it that. Words, spoken in time, doing the work locks can't. Accountability at the heart of it. Humans humaning.

A door closes closely — loud, then an echo chasing it. I'm standing in the hallway now, the one part of the building the morning hasn't reached yet. Darkest, quietest. The windows further down let in a thin, forgiving light. I'm alone here. I'm always alone here, in this particular minute, before the day asks anything of me.

Alone until I make myself present to the people of the building. That's the job, underneath the job. And presence, it turns out, has a cost nobody warns you about. I live among them, but I can't let anything go the way a neighbor is allowed to. Every leak becomes something I carry. Every broken heater, every noise complaint, every argument through a shared wall, every tenant's worst month — I take it in like it's mine to answer for, because in some real sense, it is.

The villain was never "the resident." It's whatever threatens the Vow. The lease. The contract. My word, if I've given it, which I have. A man's word should be his bond — I suppose I'd extend that to anyone made in that same image. We're given words to use. The least we can do is protect them.

None of this is really mine, if I'm honest. Not the building, not the trust, not even the discipline it takes to keep both intact. It was handed to me the way a portion gets handed to a servant before the master leaves for a while — no instructions beyond an expectation that it'll be worth more, or at least no worse, when he returns. Bury it and it stays safe. Work it and it multiplies, but only because somebody stayed up late treating it like it mattered. I've never believed discipline was a talent. It's a decision, repeated until it looks like one. Showing up sharpens more than a building. It sharpens the man doing the showing up — and if I've done any of this right, it sharpens whoever's on the other side of the door too. Iron on iron. That's the whole trade.

"Can I actually do this," I asked myself once, standing in that same hallway.

My feet begin to move. Towards the light.

Can a good man sustain being needed by ninety families without disappearing into the role himself?

I knew the answer before I finished asking. I already know it every morning the alarm turns a dream into a sound and the sound turns into a day.

They need me to show them they're safe. That we're going to be okay. That there's a version of tomorrow worth signing on for.

That's the only pitch I've got. Its ok to Renew. Maybe that's the only review that matters in the end — not five stars.

But lets be real…Land Man loves all his stars. Everyone loves the stars.
